Commonly confused words

English is full of pairs that sound alike or look alike and mean different things. Even careful writers stop at "affect or effect?" and "its or it's?". A spelling checker will not save you, because both words are spelt correctly; only the meaning tells them apart.

Below are thirty of the pairs that cause the most trouble in school essays, emails and examination papers in India. For each pair you will find the difference in one line and an example of each word in use. The memory hints are our own; if one helps, keep it, and if not, make a better one.

The best way to use this page is not to read it straight through. Find the three or four pairs that you know you get wrong, write two sentences of your own for each word, and come back for the next few when those are safe.

  • accept / except

    "Accept" is to receive or agree. "Except" means leaving out.

    Please accept this gift. Everyone came except Ravi.

  • advice / advise

    "Advice" is the noun (a thing you give). "Advise" is the verb (what you do).

    She gave me good advice. I advise you to start early.

  • affect / effect

    "Affect" is usually a verb: to influence. "Effect" is usually a noun: the result.

    The rain affected the match. The rain had a bad effect on the pitch.

  • its / it's

    "Its" shows belonging. "It's" is short for "it is" or "it has".

    The dog wagged its tail. It's raining.

  • your / you're

    "Your" shows belonging. "You're" is short for "you are".

    Is this your bag? You're late.

  • their / there / they're

    "Their" shows belonging; "there" is a place; "they're" is "they are".

    Their house is there. They're at home.

  • lose / loose

    "Lose" (one o) is to misplace or be defeated. "Loose" (two o's) is not tight.

    Do not lose the key. This shirt is loose.

  • then / than

    "Then" is about time. "Than" compares.

    Finish your work, then play. She is taller than me.

  • quiet / quite

    "Quiet" is silent. "Quite" is fairly or completely.

    Please be quiet. The film was quite good.

  • principal / principle

    A "principal" heads a school (your "pal"). A "principle" is a rule or belief.

    The principal spoke first. Honesty is a good principle.

  • stationary / stationery

    "Stationary" is not moving. "Stationery" (with e, as in envelope) is paper and pens.

    The bus was stationary. I bought stationery for school.

  • weather / whether

    "Weather" is rain and sunshine. "Whether" means "if".

    The weather is pleasant. I do not know whether he will come.

  • desert / dessert

    A "desert" is dry, sandy land. "Dessert" (double s, as in sweet stuff) is the sweet dish.

    Camels cross the desert. We had kheer for dessert.

  • beside / besides

    "Beside" is next to. "Besides" is in addition to.

    Sit beside me. Besides Hindi, she speaks Tamil.

  • borrow / lend

    You "borrow" from someone (take). You "lend" to someone (give).

    May I borrow your pen? Please lend me your pen.

  • hear / here

    "Hear" (it has "ear" in it) is with the ears. "Here" is this place.

    Can you hear me? Come here.

  • complement / compliment

    "Complement" completes. A "compliment" is praise.

    The chutney complements the dosa. She paid me a compliment.

  • fewer / less

    "Fewer" for things you can count. "Less" for amounts you cannot count.

    Fewer students came today. Add less sugar.

  • farther / further

    "Farther" for real distance. "Further" for more, in addition.

    The station is farther than the market. Do you have any further questions?

  • lay / lie

    You "lay" something down (it needs an object). You "lie" down yourself.

    Lay the book on the table. I want to lie down.

  • rise / raise

    Things "rise" by themselves. You "raise" something.

    The sun rises in the east. Raise your hand.

  • say / tell

    You "say" words. You "tell" a person.

    He said hello. He told me the news.

  • breath / breathe

    "Breath" is the noun. "Breathe" (with e) is the verb.

    Take a deep breath. Breathe slowly.

  • practice / practise

    In British and Indian English, "practice" is the noun and "practise" the verb.

    Cricket practice is at five. Practise your handwriting daily.

  • altogether / all together

    "Altogether" is completely or in total. "All together" is everyone in one group.

    That is altogether wrong. We sang all together.

  • some time / sometimes

    "Some time" is a period. "Sometimes" is now and then.

    I need some time to think. Sometimes I walk to school.

  • every day / everyday

    "Every day" is each day. "Everyday" is an adjective: ordinary.

    I read every day. These are my everyday shoes.

  • who's / whose

    "Who's" is "who is". "Whose" asks about belonging.

    Who's at the door? Whose bag is this?

  • emigrate / immigrate

    You "emigrate" from a country (exit). You "immigrate" to a country (in).

    He emigrated from India. He immigrated to Canada.

  • loose / loss

    "Loose" is not tight. "Loss" is the noun of "lose".

    A loose button. The shop made a loss.

Why these pairs are confusing

Most of them are homophones, words that sound the same but are spelt differently, such as "hear" and "here". Some are near-homophones that differ by one sound, such as "lose" and "loose". Others are a noun and a verb from the same family, where English changes one letter: advice and advise, practice and practise, breath and breathe. A useful trick for those: the noun has the "c" (as in "ice", a thing), and the verb has the "s".

Three habits that fix them

  1. Read your writing aloud, expanding the short forms. If "it is" makes sense, write "it's". If not, write "its".
  2. Keep a personal list. Everyone has their own five or six weak pairs. Write yours on a card and look at it before any important writing.
  3. Look up both words. Type each into the word finder and read the definitions side by side; seeing the two meanings together fixes the difference.
