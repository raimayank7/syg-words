How to build your English vocabulary

People try to enlarge their vocabulary by learning lists of difficult words. A month later the words are gone. The reason is simple: a word learnt from a list has no memory attached to it. A word you met in a story, looked up, and used the next day stays for life. This method is built on that difference. It takes fifteen minutes a day.

Step 1: Meet words in the wild

Read something a little above your level every day: a newspaper editorial, a magazine article, a novel, the commentary of a match. When you meet a word you do not know, do not stop. Underline it and read on. If you can still follow the passage, good; guessing from context is a skill in itself. Collect three to five words a day, no more.

Why only five? Because a word needs several meetings before it is yours. Five words a day, properly learnt, is about 1,800 words a year. Fifty words a day, forgotten by Friday, is none.

Step 2: Look the word up properly

Type the word into the word finder and read more than the first line. A proper look-up answers five questions:

  1. What part of speech is it? Noun, verb, adjective or adverb. "Present" the noun and "present" the verb are different words in practice.
  2. Which meaning fits my sentence? Common words have many senses. Read down the list until one fits the place where you met the word.
  3. How is it used? Read the example sentences. Notice the small words around it: we are "interested in", "afraid of", "good at".
  4. What are its neighbours? Look at the synonyms and the opposite. They place the word on your mental map.
  5. What is its family? If you learn "decide", collect "decision" and "decisive" with it.

Step 3: Write it down your way

Keep a small notebook. For each word write four things: the word, a meaning in your own words or in Hindi, the sentence in which you found it, and a sentence of your own about your own life. The last is the one that matters. "My grandmother is frugal; she saves every rubber band" will stay with you. "Frugal: careful with money" will not.

Step 4: Use it within a day

Say the word aloud to someone, put it in a message, or write it in your diary before you sleep. A word used once is twice as likely to be remembered as a word only read. It will feel a little forced the first time. That is fine.

Step 5: Revise on a schedule

Memory fades quickly at first and then more slowly, so revisions should be close together at the start and then spread out. Look at a new word again after one day, three days, one week, two weeks and one month. Each look takes ten seconds: cover the meaning, say it, say your sentence, check.

A simple way to run this without a calendar: write the date at the top of each notebook page, and each morning open the pages from yesterday, three days ago, last week, two weeks ago and last month.

Shortcuts that are honest

  • Learn word parts. Prefixes, suffixes and roots unlock whole families. See prefixes and suffixes.
  • Learn words in groups. Ten words for kinds of walking (stroll, march, limp, wander) stick better together than apart.
  • Upgrade the words you overuse. Notice your own "very", "nice", "good" and "thing", and find exact replacements. See words to use instead of "very".
  • Use your Hindi. A Hindi meaning is a perfectly good hook. Many word pages on this site give one.

What does not work

  • Memorising the dictionary from A. Everyone who tries stops at "abandon".
  • Learning rare words to impress. A word nobody uses will not be met again, and so will be forgotten. Learn the words you actually meet.
  • Passive collecting. Saving words in a phone and never looking at them is not learning.
  • Using a big word where a small one is better. The purpose of a large vocabulary is to choose the exact word, which is often the simple one.

How to know it is working

After a month, read an article of the kind you read at the start. You will underline fewer words. That, and nothing else, is the test.
