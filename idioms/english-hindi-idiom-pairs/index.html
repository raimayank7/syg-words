English idioms and their Hindi twins

The first rule of translating an idiom is: never translate the words. "It is raining cats and dogs" turned word by word into Hindi makes no sense, and "नौ दो ग्यारह होना" turned into English is a sum. What you translate is the idea. The pleasant surprise is how often the other language already has its own idiom for the same idea. Sometimes even the picture is the same: crocodiles weep false tears in both languages, and fire is fed with fuel in one and ghee in the other.

The table lists thirty such pairs. Use it in three ways: to translate a passage naturally, to remember an English idiom through a muhavara you already know, and to answer the "give the Hindi equivalent" question that some school papers ask.

Three kinds of match

  1. Same picture, same meaning. "Crocodile tears" and मगरमच्छ के आँसू; "walls have ears" and दीवारों के भी कान होते हैं. These are the easy ones.
  2. Different picture, same meaning. "A piece of cake" and बाएँ हाथ का खेल; "a drop in the ocean" and ऊँट के मुँह में जीरा. Most pairs are of this kind. Each language reaches for a picture from its own life: cake in one, a camel in the other.
  3. No match at all. "Break the ice" has no settled muhavara in Hindi; you translate the meaning: झिझक तोड़ना, बातचीत शुरू करना. Many idioms fall here, and a plain translation is the honest choice.

False friends

Some idioms look alike and mean different things. "To pull someone's leg" is to tease in a friendly way, but टाँग खींचना is to drag someone down or mock them. "To eat one's words" is to take back what one said, while अपनी बात से मुकरना is to deny having said it. Check the meaning, not the picture.

How to translate a sentence with an idiom

  1. Find the idiom and write its plain meaning.
  2. Ask whether the other language has an idiom for that meaning. This table and our theme pages will tell you.
  3. If it has, use it, and adjust the verb to the sentence. If it has not, write the plain meaning. Never invent an idiom.
