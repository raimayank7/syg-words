How to learn and remember idioms

Most people learn idioms the night before a test and forget them a week later. The reason is simple: they read a list. Lists do not stick. Idioms are small pictures and small stories, and they are remembered the way pictures and stories are remembered. Here is a method that needs fifteen minutes a day.

Step 1: Five a day, one theme at a time

Pick five idioms from one theme, for example five animal idioms. Idioms on one subject hold each other in memory, because the mind stores them in the same place. Five is enough. Twenty a day means none remembered.

Step 2: See the picture

For each idiom, close your eyes and see the literal picture for three seconds: a cat jumping out of a bag, a person skating on thin ice that is starting to crack. Then join the picture to the meaning: the cat is the secret that got out; the ice is the risk. This small step is what most learners skip, and it is the one that does the work.

Step 3: Write one sentence about your own life

Do not copy the example. Write a true sentence: "Last Sunday my brother let the cat out of the bag about Mother's gift." A sentence about your own family, school or street ties the idiom to a real memory. That is why you will find it again when you need it.

Step 4: Say it aloud

Speak your five sentences once. Idioms are mostly used in speech, and the tongue needs practice as much as the eye. If you learn with a friend, ask each other "what happened?" questions that have to be answered with the idiom.

Step 5: Revise on days 2, 4, 7 and 14

Memory fades fast in the first two days and then more slowly. So look at each set again on the next day, two days after that, at the end of the week and once more a week later. A revision takes one minute: cover the meaning, read the idiom, say the meaning and your sentence. A notebook with the date at the top of each page is all the equipment you need.

Five new idioms on five days of the week, with the weekend for revision and the practice quiz, comes to about a hundred idioms in a month, learnt properly.

Use both languages

If Hindi is your stronger language, the Hindi meaning under each English idiom on this site is your bridge. Better still, look for the matching muhavara. When you know that "a piece of cake" is बाएँ हाथ का खेल and "add fuel to the fire" is आग में घी डालना, both stay in memory together. Our matching pairs page lists thirty such twins.

What does not work

  • Reading long lists once. It feels like progress and leaves nothing behind.
  • Learning the meaning without a sentence. You will recognise the idiom but will not be able to use it.
  • Learning rare idioms first. Start with the ones you hear in films, news and class. They get free revision every time you hear them.
  • Translating word for word. Each language has its own picture for an idea.

For parents and teachers

Make it a game at dinner or in the last five minutes of class: one person acts out the literal picture, the others guess the idiom and use it in a sentence. Children remember what they have laughed at.
