How to use idioms in essays, letters and speeches

An idiom is like salt. A little brings out the taste; too much spoils the dish. This page shows where idioms belong in school and everyday writing, where they do not, and how to place one so that it sounds natural.

The first rule: match the idiom to the occasion

Kind of writingIdioms?Why
Story, diary, letter to a friendYes, freelyThese are personal and lively.
Speech, anchoring, debateYes, a few strong onesListeners remember a vivid line.
School essayOne or two per paragraph at mostThey show range, but the ideas must lead.
Formal letter, application, complaintAlmost neverPlain words are clearer and more polite.
Science, maths or history answerNoFacts need exact words.

Before and after

Flat: "I was very nervous before my speech. Then I spoke well and my teacher was very happy."

Better: "I had butterflies in my stomach before my speech. But once I began, the words flowed, and my teacher was over the moon."

Two idioms in two sentences is the limit. Add a third and the reader starts to notice the idioms instead of the story.

Five rules for placing an idiom

  1. Know the exact meaning. "Break the ice" is about starting a friendly talk, not about breaking a rule or a record.
  2. Keep the words fixed, change only the tense. "She let the cat out of the bag", "he will let the cat out of the bag". Never "let the cat out of the box".
  3. Let the sentence explain it. Write so that a reader who does not know the idiom can still follow: "The test was a piece of cake; I finished it in ten minutes."
  4. Do not explain it twice. "It was raining cats and dogs very heavily" says the same thing two times.
  5. Do not put idioms in quotation marks. Quotation marks make it look as if you are unsure. Use the idiom as part of your own sentence.

Idioms that suit an essay

For essays, choose idioms that are calm and widely known: "in a nutshell", "a blessing in disguise", "leave no stone unturned", "the tip of the iceberg", "sooner or later", "a race against time". They read well in serious writing. Avoid slangy or very playful ones such as "couch potato" in an essay on health; say "people who sit for long hours" instead.

Idioms in a formal letter

In an application to a principal or a complaint to an office, clarity wins. "I request you to grant me leave for two days" is right. "I am under the weather and need to call it a day" is not. One exception is the settled phrases of formal English, such as "at the earliest" and "with due respect", which are part of letter writing itself.

Idioms in a speech

A speech is heard once, so one clear idiom near the start and one near the end work best. "Rome was not built in a day, and neither was this school" opens a founder's day speech well. Pause after the idiom. Give the listeners a second to enjoy it.

A warning about clichés

Some idioms have been used so often that they have lost their shine: "at the end of the day", "think outside the box", "last but not the least". One of them will not hurt. A paragraph full of them sounds like a form letter. When an idiom comes to mind too easily, ask whether a plain, exact sentence would say more.

Practice

Take a paragraph you wrote last week. Underline one feeling and one action. Replace each with an idiom from our feelings or work pages. Read it aloud. If it sounds like you, keep it. If it sounds like a textbook, take it out.
