Idiom, proverb and phrase: what is the difference?

Students often use the three words as if they meant the same thing. They do not, and an examiner who asks for "an idiom" will not accept a proverb. Here is the difference in plain words.

A phrase

A phrase is simply a small group of words that go together and do not make a full sentence: "in the morning", "a cup of tea", "very quickly". A phrase means exactly what its words say. Every idiom is a phrase, but most phrases are not idioms.

An idiom

An idiom is a phrase whose meaning is different from the meaning of its separate words. If you "spill the beans", there are no beans; you have told a secret. If something "costs an arm and a leg", nobody loses a limb; it is expensive. You cannot work out an idiom from a dictionary, word by word. You have to learn it whole.

Two more things mark an idiom. First, it fits inside your sentence and changes with it: "she spilled the beans", "do not spill the beans", "he has spilt the beans". Second, its words are fixed. You cannot say "spill the peas" or "cost a hand and a foot".

A proverb

A proverb is a short, complete sentence that gives advice or states a truth that people have agreed on for a long time: "Honesty is the best policy." "A stitch in time saves nine." It stands on its own. You quote a proverb; you do not bend it. Nobody says "a stitch in time saved nine yesterday".

Examples side by side

  • Phrase: "under the table" in "The cat is under the table." It means what it says.
  • Idiom: "under the weather" in "I am under the weather today." It means slightly ill, and weather has nothing to do with it.
  • Proverb: "Every cloud has a silver lining." A full sentence with a message of hope.

Where people go wrong

  1. Calling every saying an idiom. "Actions speak louder than words" is a full sentence with a lesson, so it is a proverb, though many lists (and some of our theme pages, for convenience) keep such common sayings beside idioms.
  2. Changing the fixed words. "Raining dogs and cats" is wrong even though the animals are the same.
  3. Using an idiom literally. "He kicked the bucket and it rolled away" is a sentence about a bucket, not a use of the idiom.
  4. Mixing two idioms. "We will burn that bridge when we come to it" mixes "burn your bridges" with "cross that bridge when we come to it".

Is it the same in Hindi?

Yes. Hindi makes the same split: a मुहावरा is the idiom, and a लोकोक्ति or कहावत is the proverb. Our page on muhavare and lokoktiyan explains it with Hindi examples.
