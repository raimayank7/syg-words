Name in Scripts

Before you print, engrave or tattoo your name in another script

It is fun to see your name in another script, and it is natural to want to use it: on a card, a gift, a wall or your skin. This page lists what to check first. The short version: treat any tool as a first draft, and have a reader of the script check the draft before anything becomes permanent.

What can go wrong

  • A wrong spelling. A tool writes the sounds it understands. A real person who reads the script may spell the name differently, and if you print the tool's version, the reader may find it odd.
  • A hidden meaning. A sound written in one script can spell a different word with a meaning. Korean 심란 (Simran) is also an everyday word meaning troubled in the mind. Chinese characters carry meaning whatever the sound.
  • The wrong script for the language. Names in Japanese are written in katakana only when foreign. Arabic names have fixed spellings. A runic name is for fun and not a historical spelling.
  • Style. Handwritten Arabic, Urdu or Chinese calligraphy follows rules a screen font does not, and a copy of a font is not calligraphy.

For official documents

A name on a passport, a visa or a certificate in another language is spelled by the issuing authority, often following a set transliteration rule. Do not use this tool's output on an official form. Ask the office, a notary or a sworn translator which spelling to use.

For a tattoo or engraving

Show the draft to a native reader, ideally two people, and ask them to say it aloud and write it from memory. Ask the tattoo artist or engraver to show you a proof on paper. Check the direction: Arabic, Urdu and Hebrew read from right to left, and a mirrored word is a classic mistake. Do not use symbols you cannot read. We provide no advice on the meaning of any sign beyond what is written on the pages.

For a gift or a card

A card made with the picture button is fine for a gift when the recipient knows it is a draft made by a tool. Say so on the card or in the message: "my name written by sound in Japanese" is true, and avoids the claim that it is the official Japanese spelling.

Questions

Can I rely on the picture for a tattoo?

We advise against it without checking with a native reader and the artist. The picture is a convenience, not a proof.

Who can check my name?

A native reader of the script: a friend, a teacher, a community member, or a professional translator.
