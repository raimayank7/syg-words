Name in Scripts

Your name in another script: written by sound, not by meaning

When you ask for your name in Hindi, Arabic or Japanese, you are asking for transliteration, not translation. A translation changes the meaning of the words. Your name has no meaning to carry across, or its meaning is not what the name is for. Transliteration keeps the sound and changes only the writing, so that a reader of the other script can say something close to your name.

Why there is rarely one right answer

Every script has its own set of sounds. Hindi has sounds that English lacks, such as the retroflex "t", and English has sounds Hindi lacks, such as the "th" in "this". Japanese has no "l" and no single consonants without a vowel, Arabic writes no short vowels, and Russian has no "h" in the English sense. So a name has to be bent to fit. Two careful people can bend it in two ways and both be right. The Hindi names किरण and किरन are the same name. The Japanese name for "Mayank" could end in ク or ック depending on how it sounds to the writer.

What this tool does

It reads the letters you type as sounds, breaks them into consonants and vowels, and writes each sound with the closest letter of the other script, following the habits of that script. For the Indian scripts it works from Devanagari and moves every letter to its twin, because those scripts share one order of letters. For Japanese and Korean it follows the standard way foreign names are written. For Arabic and Urdu it writes consonants and long vowels, as those scripts do. The list below says which scripts are covered.

Scripts that are not here, and why

  • Chinese. A Chinese name is written with characters that carry meaning, and each person chooses them. A tool that picked characters automatically would produce nonsense or accidental meanings, so we do not offer it.
  • Tamil. Tamil has no separate letters for voiced and aspirated sounds, so one letter stands for k and g, and the right choice depends on where the sound falls in the word. A reliable rule needs a dictionary we do not have.
  • Thai, Hebrew and others need rules we could not check against an authoritative source, so they are left out rather than guessed.

How to get the best result

  1. Type the name the way it sounds. Use "aa" for a long a, "ee" for a long i, "oo" for a long u.
  2. Check the result with a reader of that script. Where the result will be printed, engraved or inked, this step is not optional.
  3. If your name is a known one, such as a king or a saint, there may be a traditional spelling in that script. Use that.

Questions

Is my name in Chinese a translation?

No. A Chinese name is written with characters chosen by a person who knows both the sound and the meaning. This tool does not produce Chinese for that reason.

Why does the same name have two spellings?

Because the sounds of one language do not fit exactly into another, so a careful writer must choose.
