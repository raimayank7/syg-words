Researching a surname: spelling, records and where to look

A list of surname counts is a starting point, not an answer. If you want to learn where your own name comes from, here is how researchers usually proceed.

1. Write down what you know

Start with living relatives: full names, birth places and dates, and how each name was spelled. Collect documents: certificates, school records, letters.

2. Expect spelling variants

Clerks wrote names as they heard them. Many families changed or simplified a name on arrival or later. Search for variants: one letter changed, a letter doubled, a different ending.

3. Use records in order

  • Birth, marriage and death records, which usually name parents.
  • Census returns, which list households.
  • Immigration, passenger and naturalisation records for migrants.
  • Wills, land records and church registers for earlier periods.

4. Check each step

Names repeat in a place, so two people with the same name may not be related. Confirm a link with two independent records before you add it to your tree.

5. DNA tests

DNA tests can suggest relatives and regions, but results come with margins of error, and companies update their estimates. Read their privacy terms before you send a sample.

6. Keep a log

Write down every search, even those that find nothing, with the date and the source. It saves repeating work and shows what is left to try.

Questions

Does this list tell me where my name came from?

No. It counts people in the United States in 2010. Origin needs records and sometimes language experts.

Should I trust a family crest for my surname?

No. Many crests sold online are not connected to any one family. Check with records.
