How to solve anagrams by hand

An anagram is a word made by rearranging all the letters of another word or of a jumble: LISTEN becomes SILENT, and the jumble RTAEW becomes WATER. A machine solves one in an instant, as our anagram solver does. Solving one in your head is a skill, and it grows quickly with a few habits. Here are the ones that help most.

1. Pull out a common ending

Look first for the letters of an ending and set them aside: -ing, -ed, -er, -est, -ly, -tion, -ness, -ment, -able, -s. With GNIREAD, take out ING and you are left with READ: READING. A long jumble becomes a short one.

2. Pull out a common beginning

Do the same with prefixes: re-, un-, de-, dis-, pre-, mis-, in-, ex-, out-, over-. With LAYREP, take out RE and look at what remains: LAYP, which is PLAY. The answer is REPLAY. If a prefix leads nowhere you have lost two seconds; put the letters back and try another.

3. Join letters that travel together

Some letters are usually found side by side. Join them and treat the pair as one piece: QU, TH, CH, SH, PH, WH, CK, NG, and the doubles EE, OO, LL, SS. In THGIN the pair TH and the group IGH almost solve it for you: NIGHT, THING.

4. Separate vowels from consonants

Write the consonants on one line and the vowels beneath. English words mostly alternate between the two, so try threading vowels between consonants. A jumble with one vowel will have consonant blends (STR, SCR, NCH); a jumble with many vowels will have vowel pairs (EA, OU, AI, IE).

5. Write the letters in a circle

Letters written in a row keep suggesting the same wrong reading. Write them round a circle, or in a random cluster, and the eye is freed to start from any letter. This is the oldest trick of crossword solvers, and it works.

6. Change the order and look again

If the circle does not help, write the letters in alphabetical order, then in reverse. Each new arrangement shows different neighbours. On paper, letter tiles or small torn squares that you can slide about are even better.

7. Start from the rare letter

A J, Q, X, Z, K or V limits the answer greatly, because few letter patterns contain it. Ask where that letter usually sits: X late in the word (EX-, -AX, -IX), J and V at the start or after a vowel, K after C or at the end.

8. Try each consonant as the first letter

Say the jumble aloud beginning with each consonant in turn, and with the common starting blends you can make. Your ear knows more words than your eye. Many solutions arrive as a sound before they arrive as a spelling.

9. Find a shorter word and grow it

If the whole word will not come, find any word of three or four letters inside the jumble, then see whether the remaining letters can be fixed to its front or back. From TRAINED you might first see RAIN, then TRAIN, then TRAINED. Our word finder lists every shorter word in a set of letters, which is good practice for this.

10. Learn the famous families

Certain sets of letters make many words, and they return again and again in puzzles: the letters A, E, R, S, T alone make aster, rates, stare, tares and tears. Knowing a few such families by heart gives you ready answers. We list the largest ones in our word list on the anagram families page.

Practice jumbles

Try these, easiest first. Each is an everyday word. Some have more than one answer; check yourself with the anagram solver.

  • KOBO, LIMK, DRIB
  • PAPEL, GITER, NAGOR
  • CHENTIK, LOOHCS, DIRNEF
  • Find a second word in the letters of GARDEN, and two more in the letters of SILENT.

When you are solving a crossword clue

In cryptic crosswords an anagram is signalled by a word that suggests mixing or damage: "confused", "broken", "wild", "off", "strange". The letters to rearrange sit next to that word, and the rest of the clue is a plain definition. Count the letters: they must match the length of the answer exactly. If you have some letters of the answer from crossing words, our pattern finder narrows the choice.
