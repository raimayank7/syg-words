Common prefixes and suffixes

A prefix is a group of letters fixed to the front of a word to change its meaning: un + happy = unhappy. A suffix is fixed to the end and usually changes the kind of word: happy + ness = happiness, a noun made from an adjective. Knowing the common ones is the fastest way to grow your vocabulary, because each prefix or suffix unlocks hundreds of words at once. It is also the best single aid for word puzzles: spot the fixed piece, and you only have to solve the short word that is left.

Prefixes that mean "not" or "opposite"

PrefixMeaningExamples
un-not; reverse an actionunfair, unkind, undo, unlock
in-, im-, il-, ir-not (the form depends on the next letter)incorrect, impossible, illegal, irregular
dis-not; apartdisagree, dishonest, disconnect
non-notnonsense, nonstop, nonfiction
mis-wronglymisspell, mislead, misplace
de-remove; reducedefrost, decode, devalue
anti-againstantiseptic, anticlockwise

Notice the spelling pattern of in-: it becomes im- before B, M and P, il- before L and ir- before R. That is why "immature" and "illegal" have double letters.

Suffixes that make nouns

  • -tion, -sion: the act or result (education, decision)
  • -ness: a state or quality (kindness)
  • -ment: an action or its result (payment)
  • -er, -or, -ist: a person who does something (teacher, actor, artist)
  • -ity: a quality (ability, equality)
  • -ship, -hood: a state or group (friendship, childhood)

Suffixes that make adjectives

  • -ful: full of (hopeful)
  • -less: without (hopeless)
  • -able, -ible: can be done (washable, visible)
  • -ous: having the quality of (famous)
  • -al, -ic: connected with (musical, poetic)
  • -y, -ish: rather like (sandy, childish)
  • -ive: tending to (active, creative)

Suffixes that make verbs and adverbs

  • -ise or -ize, -ify, -en: to make or become (modernise, simplify, widen). British and Indian spelling usually prefers -ise, American spelling -ize; our word list contains both.
  • -ly: in that manner (quickly)

The grammar endings

Four endings do the daily work of English grammar and appear on a huge share of all words: -s or -es (plurals and "he walks"), -ed (past), -ing (continuing) and the comparison pair -er and -est.

Three spelling rules that cover most cases

  1. Silent E: drop it before a suffix that starts with a vowel (hope, hoping), keep it before one that starts with a consonant (hope, hopeful).
  2. Doubling: in a short word that ends consonant, vowel, consonant, double the last letter before a vowel suffix (run, running; big, bigger). Do not double W, X or Y.
  3. Y to I: when a word ends in a consonant and Y, change Y to I before any suffix except one beginning with I (happy, happiness, happily; but carry, carrying).

How this helps with letters

Given the letters of UNHELPFUL, a solver who knows the pieces sees UN + HELP + FUL at once. To practise, type a long word into the word finder and read the list: you will see the same prefixes and endings forming again and again.
