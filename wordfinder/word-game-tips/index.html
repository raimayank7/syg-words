Word game tips: how to see more words

Whether you play a board game with letter tiles, a word puzzle in the newspaper, a spelling game on your phone or a round of "how many words can you make" at a family party, the same few skills decide how well you do. None of them is a secret, and all of them can be practised. Rules about which words count differ from game to game, so always follow the dictionary your own game names; our list is a general English word list, not the official list of any game or tournament.

Learn the short words first

Most people try to find long words and overlook the short ones, yet in almost every word game it is the two and three-letter words that let you fit a play into a tight corner or squeeze points from poor letters. Start with our list of two-letter words and their meanings. There are fewer than a hundred, and you know half of them already. Then browse the three-letter words, especially those with J, Q, X and Z.

Think in beginnings and endings

Long words are usually a short word with something fixed to the front or the back. Train yourself to see the pieces:

  • Endings: -S, -ED, -ER, -ING, -LY, -EST, -TION, -NESS, -MENT, -ABLE, -FUL, -LESS.
  • Beginnings: RE-, UN-, DE-, DIS-, IN-, MIS-, PRE-, OUT-, OVER-, NON-.

If your letters include I, N and G, set them aside and ask what verb the rest can make. Our pages on prefixes and suffixes explain each one, and the "ending in" lists show the family of each ending.

Keep a balance of vowels and consonants

In games where you hold a rack of letters and draw new ones, what you keep matters as much as what you play. A hand of all vowels or all consonants is hard to use. Try to play away doubles and awkward letters, and keep a mix. The letters of the word RETAINS are among the easiest to combine in English, which is why experienced players like to hold on to them.

Make friends with the rare letters

J, Q, X and Z frighten new players, but a short list of words tames each of them:

  • Q: nearly always needs a U. The handful of words that do not are on the Q without U page.
  • X: fits beside vowels in tiny words: ax, ex, ox, xi, and then box, fix, mix, six, tax, wax. See words with X.
  • Z: zap, zip, zoo, fizz, jazz, quiz. See words with Z.
  • J: jab, jam, jet, jog, joy, raj. See words with J.

Know what to do with too many vowels

Words made mostly of vowels are the cure for a vowel-heavy hand: area, aide, idea, oboe, audio, eerie, queue. We keep a list of vowel-heavy words. For the opposite problem there are words with no A, E, I, O or U, such as rhythm, myth and lynx.

Shuffle

If you are staring at the same letters and seeing nothing, change their order. Put them in a circle, sort them alphabetically, split vowels from consonants. A new arrangement shows new words. The methods on our anagram page all apply.

Use a word finder to learn, not to cheat

Looking up answers in the middle of a game against another person spoils it, and most groups rightly forbid it. A word finder is a training tool. After a game, type in a rack that defeated you and see what was there. Before a game, pick seven random letters and give yourself two minutes, then compare with the full list. Ten minutes of this a day does more than any amount of reading.

Settle the dictionary before you start

Arguments about whether a word "counts" have ended many friendly games. Agree on one dictionary or word list before the first move, and agree what happens when a word is challenged. If your group has no dictionary to hand, you could agree to use our word checker, on the understanding that it is a general list: it includes some unusual words and leaves out names and abbreviations.

For children and learners

Word games are one of the pleasantest ways to build vocabulary. With children, play co-operatively at first: everyone looks for words in the same letters and the family tries to beat its own record. Ask for the meaning of each new word found, and look it up together when nobody knows.
