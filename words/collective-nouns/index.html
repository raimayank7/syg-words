Collective nouns

A collective noun is a word for a group taken as one unit: a team, a family, a herd. English has a large and sometimes fanciful stock of them, with a particular word for a group of particular things. We say a flock of sheep but a herd of cattle, a bunch of keys but a bundle of sticks.

Primary school grammar asks for these, and so do fill-in-the-blank questions in many English papers. This list keeps to the forty-five that are really used in everyday English. It leaves out the rare showpieces that appear only in quiz books.

Hindi has the same idea: झुंड, टोली, गुच्छा, ढेर, क़ाफ़िला. The Hindi column gives the usual equivalent, which often serves for several English words at once.

Singular or plural verb?

This is the grammar point examiners like. A collective noun is one group, so it normally takes a singular verb: "The team is playing well." "A bunch of keys was lying on the table." Notice the second example: the verb agrees with "bunch", not with "keys".

When the members are thought of as acting separately, British and Indian English allow a plural verb: "The team are arguing among themselves." "The family have gone their different ways." If in doubt, the singular is the safer choice in an examination.

Words that are always plural

A few group words look singular but take a plural verb: "The police are looking for him." "The cattle are grazing." "People are waiting." Do not write "the police is" or "the cattles".

Choosing the right word

  • For people, the general words are group, crowd and team. The others say what kind of group it is.
  • For animals, herd is for large grazing animals, flock for birds and sheep, pack for hunters such as wolves, and swarm for insects.
  • For things, bunch is fastened at one end, bundle is tied round the middle, heap is untidy, and stack is neatly piled.
  • When no special word comes to mind, "a group of" or "a number of" is always correct.
