How to read a word page

A dictionary entry packs a great deal into a small space, and most people read only its first line. This page explains every part of a word page on this site, so that you can find the meaning you actually need and the right word for your own sentence. The same ideas apply to any good dictionary or thesaurus.

Part of speech

English words change jobs freely. "Run" is a verb in "I run every morning" and a noun in "a run of good luck". Each word page groups the meanings under noun (a name of a person, thing or idea), verb (an action or state), adjective (a describing word) and adverb (a word that tells how, when or where). Before you read the meanings, decide which job the word is doing in your sentence. That alone removes most of the wrong senses.

Senses

One word, many meanings. Each numbered item on a page is a sense: one distinct meaning. A common word such as "set", "run" or "take" has dozens. The senses are listed roughly from the most used to the least used, so the first is the best guess, but not always the right one. Read down the list, trying each sense in your sentence, until one fits. If the example sentence under a sense sounds like your sentence, you have found it.

Definition and examples

The definition says what the sense means. The examples, in quotation marks, show how the word behaves: which words stand before and after it, and in what kind of sentence it lives. When you learn a new word, learn one example with it. The example carries grammar that the definition cannot.

Synonyms

Synonyms are words that can stand for the word in that sense. This matters. "Bright" in the sense of "shining" has the synonym "brilliant"; in the sense of "clever" it has "smart". A synonym that belongs to one sense will be wrong in another. So always take a synonym from the sense you mean, never from the general list at the top.

Even within one sense, synonyms are not twins. They differ in strength (glad, happy, delighted), in tone (thin, slim, skinny) and in the company they keep (we "make" a mistake but "do" homework). Check a new synonym in its own entry before you use it.

Opposites (antonyms)

An antonym is a word of opposite meaning, again for a particular sense. "Light" is opposed to "dark" in one sense and to "heavy" in another. Not every word has an opposite; "table" and "seven" have none.

Broader and narrower words

Words are arranged in levels, from general to specific. A "dog" is a kind of "animal"; a "poodle" is a kind of "dog". On our pages these appear as "A kind of" (the broader word) and "Kinds" (narrower words). They are useful in two ways. If your word is too vague, a narrower one may be exact: not "walked" but "strolled" or "marched". If you cannot think of a word, start from the general one and look at its kinds.

Phrases with the word

Many meanings live in combinations. "Run" is ordinary; "run out", "run into" and "run-down" are separate items with their own meanings that you cannot work out from "run" alone. The phrases section lists such combinations. Learn common phrasal verbs as whole units.

Word family

These are words built from the same root: decide, decision, decisive. Learning them together multiplies your vocabulary and helps you repair a sentence. If "He took the decide" sounds wrong, the family tells you that the noun you need is "decision".

Meaning in Hindi

Where a page gives the meaning in Hindi, it lists the one to three commonest senses of the word in everyday Hindi. It is a quick bridge, not a full translation of every sense. For the finer senses, read the English definitions.

A worked example

You read: "The minister will address the gathering at noon." You know "address" as the place where someone lives, which makes no sense here. On the page for address you first note that it follows "will", so it is a verb. You skip the noun senses and read the verb senses. One of them, to speak to a person or a group, fits a minister and a gathering. Two minutes, and you have learnt that a familiar word has a second life. Most "new words" you meet are of this kind: old friends in a new sense.

Where the entries come from

The definitions, examples and word relations on the word pages are taken from WordNet, a large lexical database of English that is free for anyone to use; the details and the licence notice are on the About page. The Hindi meanings, the word lists and the guides are written by us.
