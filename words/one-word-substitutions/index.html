One-word substitutions

A one-word substitution is a single word that does the work of a whole phrase. "A person who loves books" becomes "bibliophile". "A government by the people" becomes "democracy". Such words make writing compact, and they are a regular item in school grammar and in the English sections of many competitive examinations in India.

The sixty below are among the most commonly taught. They are grouped by theme: people, beliefs and habits, government, places and collections, and general description. Many are built from Greek and Latin roots, and the roots repeat. Learn that -cide means killing, -phile means lover, -ology means study and omni- means all, and dozens of words explain themselves.

Examination patterns change. Use this list for learning, and check the current syllabus and sample papers of your own examination for what is actually asked.

How to learn them

  1. Learn by root, not by alphabet. Ten words from two roots are easier than ten unrelated ones.
  2. For each word, write the phrase from memory, then the word from the phrase. Both directions are asked.
  3. Use the word in one sentence about a real person or place you know.
  4. Revise after one day, three days and a week.
